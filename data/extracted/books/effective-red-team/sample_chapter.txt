                     This practice allows players to test strategies

            duce game theory concepts that are relevant in most real-world attacker/
            defender dynamics.

    Defining Red Teaming
                   Red teaming, put simply, is full-scope objective-based adversary emulation.
                        Many offensive security approaches, such as penetration testing, start
                   by defining a target scope (perhaps a network range, application, or specific
                   environment). Because red team operations are objective-based, they begin by
                   defining attack goals, sometimes called “trophies,” that match the objectives
                   of real adversaries. Those goals might include gaining physical access to a
                   secure data center or gaining access to sensitive information. These attack
                   trophies guide every part of the operation, including which techniques the
                   red team will use throughout the attack scenario.
                        Adversary emulation refers to how the red team mimics the tactics, tech-
                   niques, and procedures (TTPs) of attackers. Tactics are high-level attack
                   behaviors, such as initial access or lateral movement. Techniques provide a
                   more detailed description of the behavior, including specific methods like
                   phishing or exploiting public-facing applications. Procedures describe those
                   techniques in detail, even including the specific tooling and command syn-
                   tax an actor uses. A red team that emulates a specific adversary studies their
                   TTPs, just as a chess master analyzes an opponent’s patterns, to test defend-
                   ers against attack scenarios that are accurate and realistic.
                        Adversary emulation aims to replay a specific, known actor’s TTPs as
                   faithfully as possible. It focuses on what the threat actor has done previ-
                   ously, based on available threat intelligence, and closely adhering to those
                   same approaches. Its close relative, adversary simulation, drops these rigid
                   restrictions to demonstrate what a similar threat actor could do, allowing
                   testers to apply greater creativity to adapt attack activity to the nuances of
                   their specific environment. Realistically, no emulation can be perfect, since
                   intelligence is always incomplete and attackers typically change their TTPs
                   over time. Skilled red teams apply a balance between these approaches, by
                   staying faithful to an adversary’s overall intent and capabilities while adapt-
                   ing techniques to the target environment. Keep in mind that the industry
                   often uses these terms interchangeably and sometimes inconsistently. We’ll
                   use emulation generally throughout this book and make specific distinc-
                   tions wherever they are needed.
                        Let’s consider a red team operation modeled on Burly Vortex, a fic-
                   tional ransomware group we’ll be using throughout this book to represent a
                   financially motivated threat actor. A pure emulation of Burly Vortex would
                   replicate their past campaigns step by step, perhaps even using the exact
                   same phishing templates and malware payloads the group had used in past
                   breach activity. A simulation, on the other hand, might use Burly Vortex’s
                   attack playbook as a general reference, but would allow flexibility to adapt
                   any TTPs when the target environment calls for it. For example, the red
                   team might modify their approach for gaining initial access, if the original
                   path is not available, or apply different lateral movement techniques that
                   serve a similar purpose. Both simulation and emulation have unique ben-
                   efits for training defenders and validating defenses.

                Red team operations are also full-scope by default. Scope is a testing
            engagement’s set of boundaries. Most security testing operates within tightly
            drawn scopes to manage risk or focus on specific concerns. Real-world
            adversaries do not respect scope boundaries. They target any combination
            of people, facilities, systems, and third parties that accomplishes their goal.
            A red team mimics that behavior as closely as possible within legal, ethical,
            and safety constraints. In later chapters, we’ll cover how to plan your opera-
            tions in a way that maximizes results while minimizing the potential risks
            involved with emulating attackers.

            An Example Red Team Operation
            Imagine that the security team at ACME Corp, a major aerospace compo-
            nent manufacturer, has been tracking an increasing trend of ransomware
            attacks targeting the aerospace sector. Leadership has grown concerned
            about exposures to both the operational technology that runs its manu-
            facturing floors and the third-party providers that transport components
            between ACME’s facilities. The security team and its stakeholders request a
            red team operation to test how the organization would hold up against such
            an attack scenario.
                 A small, trusted insider group kicks off the initial planning process.
            The chief information security officer (CISO), a couple of senior security
            leaders, stakeholders from affected business units and a representative
            from Legal hold a meeting to discuss the operation. This team chooses
            Burly Vortex as the threat actor on which to base the operation, and sets
            three high-level attack goals similar to ones this group would actually pur-
            sue: gaining access to confidential design schematics, altering shipment
            schedules in the logistics systems, and establishing persistent access to engi-
            neering networks. The group documents formal rules of engagement that
            outline the activities the red team is authorized to perform and who to con-
            tact if escalations are required.
                 The operation runs for six weeks. The red team starts by performing
            reconnaissance and targeted social engineering against members of the
            operations and engineering teams, yielding an initial foothold into ACME’s
            internal network. The team then moves laterally and escalates privileges,
            as they move closer toward reaching the three attack trophies previously
            defined. When an attempted initial access method fails partway through
            the operation (because a vigilant defender sees it and responds), the team
            retries and adapts its approach. The red team keeps the trusted insider
            group informed throughout, but to maintain realism, does not provide any
            advance warning to the blue team, including security operations center
            (SOC) analysts and incident responders. After all, the whole point of the
            exercise is to see how the organization responds to an unexpected attack
            scenario.
                 After the operation concludes, the red team holds a series of outbrief
            sessions with different stakeholder audiences. The red team first gives
            the SOC team a detailed technical walkthrough of the attack activities to
            understand whether detective controls worked as intended (or didn’t). The

                   Red Teaming vs. Vulnerability Scanning vs. Penetration Testing
                   Red teaming is just one of many forms of offensive security testing, and it
                   isn’t always the right tool for a given job. When should you choose red team-
                   ing instead of vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, or other offensive
                   security approaches?
                        Vulnerability scanning is an automated process used to identify known
                   vulnerabilities and misconfigurations across systems, networks, and applica-
                   tions. A scanner or endpoint agent examines operating systems, open ports,
                   running services, and configurations, and compares the results against a data-
                   base of known issues. These scans can run continuously with minimal over-
                   head and even integrate seamlessly with ticketing systems to triage findings
                   to system owners. As such, vulnerability scanning is a foundational element
                   of most organizations’ ongoing security hygiene. This automation does, how-
                   ever, come with some drawbacks. Scanners tend to generate high volumes of
                   findings, including many false positives. They also rarely identify exploitable
                   chains that involve multiple steps against different services. Humans typically
                   need to validate true positives via manual efforts (although AI is beginning to
                   reduce this dependency significantly).
                        Penetration testing, by comparison, applies some level of automation but
                   relies heavily on creativity and judgment to chain multiple vulnerabilities
                   together and illustrate what an attacker could accomplish with the find-
                   ings discovered. Given this additional level of analysis and decision-making
                   required, it’s more expensive than scanning and produces far fewer find-
                   ings, but those it does present are typically much more accurate and contex-
                   tualized. Penetration tests usually operate within a predefined scope that is
                   technical-only, which leaves other aspects of an attack surface (like humans,
                   physical controls, process gaps, and organizational blind spots) untested.
                        Red teaming’s scenario-driven, full-scope approach is the most expen-
                   sive and time-consuming of the three methodologies, but it’s also uniquely
                   able to determine how well an organization could hold up against a moti-
                   vated, capable adversary.
                        To illustrate the differences, consider how each approach might assess
                   a bank’s SWIFT environment. (SWIFT is the global messaging network
                   banks use to authorize interbank transfers, which makes it a target for
                   financially motivated attackers.) A vulnerability scan would quickly and
                   efficiently identify known weaknesses in the underlying systems, producing
                   a list of common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVE) IDs and risk scores.
                   A penetration test would find exploitable vulnerabilities and demonstrate
                   how an attacker might chain them together to compromise the SWIFT
                   infrastructure.

                    A red team operation would start from the attacker’s goal of moving

                might begin by performing targeted phishing and vishing of payment opera-

                the bank’s detection and response teams catch the malicious activity. Its find-

                whether the bank’s end-to-end defenses can withstand a realistic attack.


  Learning from Game Theory
                Game theory is a branch of mathematics focused on the study of strategic
                interactions among rational decision-makers. It gives us formal ways to rea-
                son about decisions that depend on what other actors do and about the out-
                comes that result when each side chooses its moves based on what it believes
                the other sides know and want. For red teamers, game theory is most useful
                as an analytical lens. Cybersecurity is fundamentally a domain of strategic
                interaction between attackers and defenders, and many of the dynamics red
                teams encounter in the field have been studied formally for decades.
                    Four game theory concepts are particularly relevant to red teamers:
                zero-sum games, which describe scenarios where one side’s gain is the other
                side’s loss; information asymmetry, where one side knows things the other
                doesn’t; signaling games, where actors convey information to influence the
                other side’s decisions; and bounded rationality, which recognizes that real
                decision-makers work under constraints and can’t evaluate every option.
                Each of these concepts gives red teamers a sharper way to think about how
                attacks actually unfold and why defenses succeed or fail.

                Zero-Sum Games
                Cybersecurity contains plenty of genuinely zero-sum moments. When an
                adversary exfiltrates data or disrupts operations, the attacker gains what
                the organization loses. Red team engagements, however, are not zero-sum.
                Although the red team and the blue team are notionally on opposing sides
                of an exercise, they work toward the same goal of strengthening their orga-
                nization’s security program. A red team’s success at compromising a criti-
                cal system undetected is a win for the blue team, which gains information
                about which parts of the organization’s detection and response process
                need work.
                     Red teams, too, benefit from running their simulations against capable,
                well-instrumented defenders. Each engagement sharpens their understand-
                ing of how modern detection stacks behave and how response playbooks
                execute under pressure. A well-run red team program generates results that
                neither side could achieve alone.

                   Information Asymmetry
                   Information asymmetry, which describes situations in which one party has more
                   or better information than another, shapes attacker-defender dynamics
                   more than any other game theory concept in this chapter.
                        The Battle of Midway during World War II shows the power of informa-
                   tion asymmetry. On the morning of June 4, 1942, Japanese Admiral Chūichi
                   Nagumo had launched a first wave of aircraft against Midway Atoll, a stra-
                   tegically vital American outpost in the central Pacific. The strike damaged
                   facilities across the island, but the wave’s commander radioed back that a
                   second attack would be needed. Nagumo had held a second wave of aircraft
                   in reserve on his carriers’ hangar decks, armed with anti-ship weapons
                   in case American carriers appeared. With no American ships yet sighted,
                   he ordered the reserve aircraft re-armed with land-attack bombs to fin-
                   ish the job on Midway. In the middle of re-arming, a scout plane reported
                   American ships to the northeast, including a carrier.
                        This situation became known as Nagumo’s Dilemma. He could halt the
                   re-arming and restore anti-ship weapons on the reserve aircraft for an imme-
                   diate launch. The strike would go out piecemeal, without fighter escort, and
                   the first-wave aircraft now returning low on fuel would have nowhere to land.
                   Alternatively, he could recover the first wave of returning planes, re-arm
                   everything for a coordinated anti-ship strike, and launch roughly 45 minutes
                   later. Nagumo chose the coordinated option, as Japanese naval doctrine
                   encouraged. While his flight decks were full of fueled aircraft and ordnance
                   staged from the re-arming reversal, American dive bombers found him.
                   Three of his four carriers were fatally hit within minutes, and the fourth fell
                   that afternoon. The Imperial Navy never recovered its offensive footing in
                   the Pacific.
                        The Americans had been reading Japanese naval communications for
                   weeks. They knew Midway was the target, they knew Nagumo’s approximate
                   position and timetable, and they had positioned three carriers in ambush
                   before he ever launched his first strike. Nagumo was making consequential
                   decisions under uncertainty about whether American carriers even existed
                   in the area.
                        In cybersecurity scenarios, you may assume that the attacker holds
                   the information advantage. After all, they choose when to strike, which
                   techniques to use, and what objectives to pursue, while the defender has
                   to prepare for a wide range of possibilities. However, within mature secu-
                   rity organizations, defenders gain significant advantages of their own that
                   can give them an upper hand in attack scenarios. Defenders know their
                   environment, including which systems exist and what traffic flows and
                   processes are normal and abnormal.
                        Claude Shannon described this dynamic in what’s now called Shannon’s
                   Maxim, which states that systems should be built under the assumption that
                   the adversary understands their general design. Details about systems can
                   leak in many ways that defenders can never fully control, including through
                   ill-advised human decisions. Security that relies on design details remain-
                   ing hidden can fall apart the moment they are exposed. Assuming the

                                AI AND THE AT TACKER’S LE ARNING CURV E

                          A recent dynamic that is important to watch carefully is how AI tools are
                          changing a defender’s advantage in knowing their network. Historically,
                          attackers had to invest considerable time learning about the target environ-
                          ment, reading through internal documentation, mapping systems, and build-
                          ing a mental model of the business before performing further attack activity.
                          AI-assisted analysis is compressing that timeline significantly. Today, an
                          attacker with access to internal wikis, ticket systems, or code repositories
                          can use language models to synthesize large volumes of organizational
                          knowledge quickly and can ask targeted questions to learn where sensitive
                          systems are or how specific business processes work. While the defender’s
                          information advantage still exists, AI is enabling attackers to close the gap.
                          Red teams should increasingly model and test this dynamic.



                    Well-designed red team engagements help defenders test and strengthen
                their asymmetry advantages. An engagement can serve to validate whether a
                large defender advantage exists or whether it’s only nominal.

                Signaling Games
                Signaling games describe interactions in which one party conveys information,
                whether truthfully or deceptively, to influence another party’s decisions.
                Signaling is relevant to attacker-defender dynamics because the conclusions
                each side draws about the other depend heavily on the signals they observe.
                An attacker who can manipulate the signals a defender relies on gains a
                powerful operational advantage; a defender who can recognize manipulated
                signals, or plant signals that mislead an attacker, gains the same.
                     Consider a campaign one of our teams once ran. We created a fictional
                recruiter persona on LinkedIn, purportedly looking to fill roles at the target
                company. Over the course of three weeks, we built that persona’s network,
                connecting with hundreds of people in the organization, and otherwise
                behaved as a normal recruiter would. After the persona had established
                itself, we began directly reaching out to specific employees at the target
                company with links to job postings hosted on its official website. The first
                message we sent included a link to a real opportunity. So did the second,

                    which we sent a week later. By the third message, the recipient had deter-

                    roles. That third link went to a posting on infrastructure we controlled and
                    required the recipient to take a small action to view it. The recipient com-

                         Every signal we sent in the early phase was authentic. The deception lay
                    in the channel itself, not in the messages. The early authentic content was
                    an investment in credibility, ensuring that a single deceptive signal at the
                    end would land without raising suspicion.
                         Defenders use similar techniques, such as honeypots and deception networks:
                    signaling exercises in which defenders construct systems that present them-
                    selves as legitimate, valuable production assets to anyone reconnoitering the
                    environment. These systems incorporate signals like realistic naming conven-
                    tions and believable service banners. When attackers act on those signals,
                    they reveal their TTPs while gaining nothing operationally useful in return.
                         A red team engagement might evaluate whether anomalous traffic
                    patterns get flagged or dismissed, or whether a slow drip of low-severity
                    alerts reaches the threshold necessary for someone to investigate further.
                    Adversary emulation that blends into expected baseline activity is par-
                    ticularly valuable because it closely replicates real attacks.

                    Bounded Rationality
                    The economist and cognitive scientist Herbert Simon coined the term
                    bounded rationality in the 1950s to describe how decisions actually get made.
                    Decision-makers work with limited time and information, and they choose
                    among the options they can identify rather than searching exhaustively.
                    Nagumo’s Dilemma exemplifies this concept well, as Nagumo had to com-
                    mit to a choice under intense time pressure and with limited information,
                    knowing his decision would be irreversible once it was made.
                         Bounded rationality has direct applications in red teaming. Engagements
                    should always be grounded in what real adversaries are likely to do given the
                    constraints they face, instead of what they theoretically could do with infinite
                    time and budget. An attack path that requires a year of dedicated reconnais-
                    sance or an exorbitant amount of AI token cost is unlikely to model the true
                    threat the organization faces. A useful red team operation tests against what
                    is feasible and probable for adversaries to do.
                         Defenders can also use bounded rationality to their advantage. Adding
                    custom detection logic and additional depth of countermeasures can raise
                    the cost and feasibility of an attack. When costs climb high enough, many
                    attackers will abandon the effort to pursue softer targets.


      A Red Team’s Mindset
                    Red teaming is a craft, and like any craft, it depends as much on how the
                    practitioner thinks as on what they know. Operations almost never go com-
                    pletely according to plan. Operators hit snags when compiling payloads or

                maintaining persistent access. The greatest challenge for red teamers is often
                staying focused when things go wrong. The following principles describe the
                mental models that lead great operators to produce top-quality work.

                Principle 1: Do No Harm
                In red teaming, “do no harm” starts with the obvious. Don’t crash produc-
                tion systems. Don’t destroy data or make irreversible changes. At a deeper
                level, it also means never inadvertently degrading the organization’s secu-
                rity posture or burning stakeholder trust.
                      Suppose you’ve just gained access to a database that contains sensitive
                HR information. It would be quick and easy to just dump the entire table
                to provide irrefutable proof of your access. But should you? Providing a
                row count, plus a single redacted row, could demonstrate the same level
                of access without the risk of exposing the full dataset. As a rule of thumb,
                always take on the minimum level of risk required to make your point, even
                if it means performing a few extra steps.
                      Doing no harm also means working with legal, compliance, and HR
                partners throughout initial planning stages, especially when an operation
                will touch sensitive areas of the organization. Treating those partners as
                collaborators ultimately results in safer and faster operations. In the long
                term, a collaborative, disciplined approach builds trust, allowing red teams
                to continue doing interesting and consequential work.

                Principle 2: Trust but Verify
                One of the most valuable traits in red team tradecraft is healthy skepticism.
                Organizations operate with a long list of unstated assumptions about how
                their controls are configured and their environments are protected. Many
                of those assumptions don’t hold up under scrutiny. Red teams are well-
                positioned to find the gaps between an organization’s perception and its
                reality.
                    Say a security organization claims to have universal multi-factor authenti-
                cation coverage. In practice, certain legacy applications may predate the cov-
                erage’s rollout, or forgotten service accounts may never have been enrolled.
                The same pattern commonly shows up in cloud security. Leadership might
                assert that secure-by-default policies protect every cloud asset, but a red team
                often finds publicly exposed storage buckets and over-permissioned roles
                that prove the assertion incomplete.
                    The principle applies to the team’s own work, too. Just because a tech-
                nique succeeded in a previous engagement doesn’t mean it will work again,
                and just because an attack path looks viable doesn’t mean it’s the best one.
                Red teams should pressure-test their own assumptions just as carefully as
                they check the organization’s to produce reliable findings and model the
                evidence-based intellectual rigor they want the rest of the organization to
                practice.

                    Principle 3: Embrace Uncertainty and Complexity
                    Red team operations rarely follow a clean, predictable script. The environ-
                    ments we test are shaped by years of questionable architectural decisions,
                    layered acquisitions, inconsistent documentation, and sometimes organic
                    weirdness. To work in that kind of terrain, red teamers must be adaptable
                    and comfortable making decisions when they can’t fully know the situation.
                        Consider a common scenario: A red team plans to deliver a phishing
                    payload via a link to a file on a common cloud storage platform (such as
                    Google Drive). The tactic has worked in past engagements. This time,
                    though, the target organization has URL filtering in place that blocks out-
                    bound access to that platform, and the link gets stripped before it reaches
                    the recipient. The original plan won’t work. The team has to decide what to
                    do next and needs to make that decision quickly enough that the operation
                    doesn’t stall. Maybe the answer is to pursue a different delivery mechanism
                    or another initial access path altogether. The judgment call must happen
                    with whatever information the team has at that moment.
                        Such pivots are commonplace during red teaming. Being attached to a
                    specific plan, or assuming the environment will behave like the last one did,
                    can derail an operation. Red teams that have internalized this principle will
                    plan every engagement thoroughly but hold the plan loosely. They expect
                    things to go sideways and adapt, continually moving toward the mission’s
                    goal even when obstacles force path changes.

                    Principle 4: Stay on Target
                    Red team operations are full of rabbit holes and time sinks. A misbehaving
                    payload or a troublesome security control can pull an operator’s attention
                    away from what is most important. It’s easy to get absorbed in solving an
                    immediate problem and lose track of an engagement’s broader purpose.
                         The strongest red teams operate with dual awareness. They zoom in to
                    handle the technical detail in front of them, then zoom out to make sure
                    that detail is actually contributing to the engagement’s objectives. No mat-
                    ter how clever the exploit, it can be a real distraction if it doesn’t move the
                    operation toward its goal.
                         Staying mission-focused requires periodic self-examination during an
                    operation. Is the current attack path still aligned with the engagement’s
                    objectives? Would alternative approaches provide a better return for the
                    time invested? Is the current technique the right fit or just the most famil-
                    iar one? An effective red team knows when to push deeper into a path and
                    when to step back and reassess.

                    Principle 5: Challenge Your Cognitive Biases
                    Red teaming is as much a mental game as a technical one. Human think-
                    ing isn’t immune to error, and the same shortcuts that help us move fast in
                    everyday life can quietly lead us off course in complex operations. In red
                    team work, where stakes are high and information is incomplete, the short-
                    cuts we don’t notice tend to be the ones that hurt us the most.

                     A few cognitive biases come up regularly in red team operations:
                     Confirmation bias We tend to seek out information that supports
                     what we already believe. A red teamer convinced that a target is vulner-
                     able to Kerberoasting may concentrate their effort there and ignore evi-
                     dence to the contrary. Strong teams test multiple hypotheses, especially
                     when an early one seems too convenient. As AI testing has entered red
                     teams’ work, this trap has become especially common. While a web
                     application generally returns the same response to the same request,
                     large language models are non-deterministic and may respond differ-
                     ently each time, so a prompt technique that fails on the first attempt
                     can succeed on a later one.
                     Anchoring bias First impressions can disproportionately shape our
                     later interpretations. If a command-and-control beacon gets flagged
                     early in an operation, it’s easy to conclude that the defenders are highly
                     capable and shift into overly stealthy techniques that waste time. The
                     early information may not reflect the broader picture, so always con-
                     sider later evidence on its own merits.
                     Groupthink When consensus forms too quickly, it can stifle dissent-
                     ing ideas. Teams that explicitly invite constructive pushback will pro-
                     duce better ideas and avoid operational dead ends that members may
                     otherwise have been afraid to question.
                     Dunning-Kruger effect People with limited knowledge or skill in a
                     domain tend to overestimate their own competence because they lack the
                     experience to recognize what they don’t yet know. Early in a red teamer’s
                     career, a few successful operations can produce a misleading sense of
                     mastery, leading to risky decisions and skipped precautions. The opposite
                     tendency also shows up at the senior end of the spectrum, where expe-
                     rienced operators sometimes don’t realize how much of what they know
                     isn’t obvious to others. Awareness of this effect helps in both directions.
                     Sunk cost fallacy People often keep investing in an unproductive
                     path because of the time they already spent on it. Teams that allocate
                     a fixed amount of time for exploring each attack path and review their
                     progress regularly are better positioned to abandon dead ends and
                     redirect their effort toward something more productive.
                    It’s unrealistic to think that we can eliminate bias from our thinking.
                We can, however, anticipate situations in which biases may surface and
                apply processes around those situations to keep our assumptions in check.

                Principle 6: Know the Adversary
                Adversary emulation requires getting into the mindset of a real threat actor
                and making decisions the way they would. The better the red team under-
                stands the adversary, the more credible their operation becomes.
                    The closest analog is method acting. A great actor goes above and beyond
                delivering lines by studying how the character thinks, what motivates them,
                how they speak, and how they move. For red teamers, a useful structure for

                    quickly encrypting data and creating leverage for payment, which means
                    the red team should prioritize initial access, fast lateral movement, and
                    reaching encryptable data quickly. A nation-state actor pursuing long-term
                    access cares about staying undetected for months or years, which means the
                    red team should emphasize operational security, patient reconnaissance,
                    and persistence mechanisms that survive defensive turnover. Different
                    adversaries change the shape of operations.

                    Principle 7: Embrace Teamwork
                    Red teaming is collaborative by nature. The work spans social engineering,
                    physical access, cloud abuse, lateral movement, malware development, and
                    many other domains. No single practitioner is likely to master all of these skills.
                         Effective red teams amplify each other’s strengths. When a cloud spe-
                    cialist works alongside a phishing operator, or when a physical tester hands
                    off a foothold to an infrastructure specialist, the engagement becomes
                    capable of attack paths that an individual may not have found alone. For
                    example, a physical tester could infiltrate a secured office space and plant
                    a device on the internal network, and a teammate could then use that foot-
                    hold to deploy malware that exfiltrates data through a cloud channel.
                         Teamwork can also make an operation safer. It’s easy to misfire com-
                    mands and misconfigure payloads when working under pressure. Having a
                    second set of eyes, whether through screen sharing, peer review, or a quick
                    sanity check before hitting enter, prevents small mistakes from becoming
                    outages or burned engagements.
                         Collaboration should extend beyond the red team itself. Red teams
                    that build constructive working relationships with the blue team and the
                    broader security organization produce better engagements. An overly com-
                    petitive relationship with other teams can undermine growth and progress.

                    To emulate an adversary credibly, the red team has to understand the envi-
                    ronment it’s operating in and the defenses inside it. For example, a red
                    teamer who understands how an endpoint detection and response (EDR)

                Principle 9: Keep Trying
                Red teaming is full of setbacks. Payloads get flagged, credentials don’t work,
                access gets cut off, and you’ll spend hours troubleshooting a problem only
                to find a missing slash in a URL. These moments can be demoralizing, but
                they’re also where growth happens.
                     Persistence is one of the most underrated traits in a red teamer. The trick
                is to stay engaged and curious, working on the problem even when things
                don’t go your way. Persistence and brute force are different skills, though,
                and pushing blindly into a failing approach burns time and attention. The
                skill is knowing when to step back, treat a failed attack path as a useful data
                point, and try a different angle. What did failure tell you? What’s a different
                approach that could work?
                     Persistence also includes taking care of yourself. This work is mentally
                and emotionally demanding, and it’s easy to internalize the pressure to
                solve every problem on your own. Burnout doesn’t produce better results.
                The operators who sustain long careers in this field pace themselves and
                recognize rest as part of the process. When you feel stuck, talk to a team-
                mate, take a break, or try something completely different. Some of the best
                breakthroughs come immediately after the point where you almost gave up.
                     Red teaming rewards consistency. Keep showing up and learning, and
                you’ll build the skills and character that sustain a long career in this field.

                Principle 10: Never Stop Learning
                Red teaming changes constantly. Today’s latest technologies will become
                tomorrow’s legacy systems. Modern defensive methods and controls replace
                outdated approaches. Threat actors continually invent new tactics and tech-
                niques. The practitioners who stay relevant are the ones who treat learning
                as part of the job.

                The Box and TryHackMe provide lab environments in which to practice
                techniques and sharpen your technical skills. Threat intelligence reports
                and offensive research blogs can help you track how attack tradecraft is

      Summary
                    Red teaming is first and foremost a mindset and a mission. In this chapter,
                    we examined how it compares with other forms of security testing, empha-
                    sizing its objective-driven, full-scope, and context-aware nature. Through
                    real-world examples and game theory principles like zero-sum dynamics,
                    information asymmetry, and bounded rationality, we framed red teaming
                    as a strategic tool that helps organizations shift from reactive to proactive
                    defense postures. We also outlined the mindset required to operate effec-
                    tively in this role, discussing the need to challenge assumptions, embrace
                    ambiguity, and prioritize integrity.
                         As we move into the next chapters, we’ll build on this foundation and
                    explore how to design, lead, and scale effective red team programs that
                    deliver lasting rewards across an organization.
