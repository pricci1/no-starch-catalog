                   The strategies in this part will help you find an entry point quickly. Try
               a shortcut, search the code, search the internet, ask someone else, attach a
               debugger, or look at a crash dump.


     4. Try Shortcuts
                    How? Based on your experience, you can make educated guesses
                    about which areas of your program’s source relate to your bug’s symp-
                    toms. You can also check systems that have repeatedly had bugs and
                    confirm they’re not at fault, initially ignoring the ones with responsibili-
                    ties that probably don’t relate to your particular problem.
                    Why? Once you know you need to jump to a specific point in your
                    program’s sources, getting there doesn’t take much time: With most
                    integrated development environments (IDEs) or editors, it takes only
                    a moment to jump from line 20 to line 10,438 in a file. One method
                    of identifying the part you need is narrowing down the target area by
                    ruling out larger sections. If you can disregard most of the program’s
                    sources, you’ll have a better idea of where to look for your target line.
                    The presented shortcuts can work either way: discovering that a system
                    is related to your bug or ruling it out.
               Shortcuts are alluring. It’s difficult to resist skipping all the hard work and
               getting a task done a lot quicker than your team would typically expect.
               However, you must be careful not to fall into the trap of repeatedly trying
               to find shortcuts without making any progress. Likewise, it’s easy to get too
               focused on one where the line of inquiry doesn’t lead to the spot that’s at
               fault. If shortcuts don’t quickly get you where you need to go, they slow you
               down because they keep you from beginning detailed investigations. In the
               worst case, you’re mistaken and spend time looking into a supposed lead
               that turns out to be a dead end.
                    Throughout your work, keep checking to make sure you’re on the
               right track. Does the spot you found via a shortcut really fit the bug you’re
               working on? Is it reasonable to conclude that this code area is related to
               the observed symptoms? Or might this code be unrelated, meaning you
               can rule out this area as the source of the problem? Make sure to stay
               focused.

               Get Information from a Crash Dump
               Acquiring a crash dump for a bug you’re working on is almost as good as
               catching it in the debugger yourself. You can load the file into your debug-
               ger and “run” it there to get most of the same information (most impor-
               tantly the call stack). Some crash dumps may contain variable values and
               program memory, which is hugely valuable but increases file size. If yours
               doesn’t, you have to go by the logic’s state and guess which variable may
               have had which value.
                   The biggest drawback of crash dumps is that they’re not interactive.
               You can’t see how the logic evolves over time. If you were actually debug-
               ging, you would potentially have stepped through what came before, and
               you might be able to continue or manipulate the instruction pointer to hop
               over the problematic code. However, if you had only caught the hardware
               exception when it had already been thrown, you wouldn’t be able to con-
               tinue past it anyway.
                   What you see in the debugger may also be challenging to analyze
               because the displayed code isn’t necessarily what’s being executed. Crash
               dumps often stem from end-user builds created with optimizations enabled,
               which means that the compiler transformed the code quite a bit. If you
               repeatedly observe crashes in a specific function but can’t figure out what’s
               going on, it may be worthwhile to disable optimizations for that specific
               function and ship a build with this change (see “Step Through the Code”
               on page 100), being careful about resulting performance issues.

               Implement Crash Dumps
               First, you must decide the right time to introduce crash dump support to
               your program. Since the goal of doing so is typically to catch users’ issues, it
               may happen only shortly before release.
                    However, having crash dumps in place early during development is also
               hugely beneficial. It makes it easier for you to weed out crashes before the
               release and allows your testers to provide more hard data when creating
               bug reports (see “What Makes a Great Bug Report?” on page 11). Having
               too many crashes during development also slows down the whole team.
                    Adding support isn’t much work in the sense of writing a lot of code
               because it doesn’t require you to create complicated new systems, define
               data structures, or implement complicated algorithms. The complexity
               comes from dealing with highly platform-specific features that vary between
               operating systems and hardware architectures.
                    Additionally, you’re operating outside the realm of functionality a pro-
               gram typically tackles. You have to write code that can run after your pro-
               gram has caused a catastrophic error and work flawlessly when there’s not
               much in the environment it can rely on.

 Implement Crash Dumps for Your Platform
 Implementing crash dumps is highly platform-dependent, with significant
 differences between various operating systems on PCs, mobile devices, and
 proprietary devices. This section shows an example using C on Windows.
     The first step is to react to the crash. Very early on in your main func-
 tion, you have to set up a handler for unhandled exceptions:

 int main(int argc, char** argv) {
     SetUnhandledExceptionFilter(handle_exception);
 --snip--

    The handle_exception() function implements the creation of the crash
 dump, which also conveniently stores the data in a file:

 #include <windows.h>
 #include <Dbghelp.h>
 #include <tchar.h>

 typedef BOOL (WINAPI *MINIDUMPWRITEDUMP)(HANDLE hProcess,
     DWORD dwPid, HANDLE hFile, MINIDUMP_TYPE DumpType,
     CONST PMINIDUMP_EXCEPTION_INFORMATION ExceptionParam,
     CONST PMINIDUMP_USER_STREAM_INFORMATION UserStreamParam,
     CONST PMINIDUMP_CALLBACK_INFORMATION CallbackParam);

 LONG WINAPI handle_exception(struct _EXCEPTION_POINTERS* exception_info) {
     HMODULE lib = LoadLibrary(_T("dbghelp.dll"));
     MINIDUMPWRITEDUMP pDump =
          (MINIDUMPWRITEDUMP)GetProcAddress(lib, "MiniDumpWriteDump");

     HANDLE file = CreateFile(_T("mydump.dmp"), GENERIC_WRITE,
         FILE_SHARE_WRITE, NULL, CREATE_ALWAYS,
         FILE_ATTRIBUTE_NORMAL, NULL);

     _MINIDUMP_EXCEPTION_INFORMATION ExInfo;
     ExInfo.ThreadId = GetCurrentThreadId();
     ExInfo.ExceptionPointers = exception_info;
     ExInfo.ClientPointers = FALSE;

     pDump(GetCurrentProcess(), GetCurrentProcessId(),
         file, MiniDumpNormal, &ExInfo, NULL, NULL);
     CloseHandle(file);

     return EXCEPTION_CONTINUE_SEARCH;
 }

 in a debugger and look at what has happened. As it is, the process involves
 asking the users or testers to share the file with you for every incident.
      An ideal solution would share it automatically. However, the more logic
 that must run in the event of a crash, the more can go wrong. Imagine your
 program operates on a device that runs out of memory, and the memory

               allocation function returns a null pointer. Because you don’t check the

                    Having to run extensive logic in a catastrophic event isn’t a great idea.
               That’s why there’s a more reliable but also more complex solution: You
               can implement your crash dump handler as an out-of-process handler.
               Instead of doing any extensive computing, the crashed program delegates
               the crash handling to another process. This may spawn when a crash takes
               place or run continuously as a kind of watchdog. The latter option simpli-
               fies response when a program does fail, because you can fully prepare the
               handler and have all the memory it requires reserved in advance. Then it
               just needs to create the crash dump and perform any further processing. (It
               would also be nice to implement a little UI where the user could describe
               what they were doing when the crash occurred.)

               Generate and Store Symbol Files
               The significant advantage of crash dumps is that they work with end-user
               builds, but those are typically stripped builds, meaning the executable no
               longer contains any debugging information. You won’t see any variable
               names, function names, source filenames, or line numbers, but only assem-
               bly instructions and memory addresses. That’s still better than nothing, but
               it can be very hard or impossible to figure out what part of your source code
               is to blame when you have such limited information.
                    While your executable should get stripped during the process of pre-
               paring a distribution build, you should also make sure to generate a symbol
               file that solely contains the otherwise missing debugging information. You
               may even have multiple symbol files for a single build, typically one for
               each executable and one for each shared library (.dll or .so files). You must
               archive these so you have them readily available if you need to debug a
               crash dump.
                    One symbol file is only ever associated with one particular build, so
               make sure you know which belongs to which. Assuming that the builds you
               distribute have a version number, a working simple approach is to label a
               directory accordingly and store the files there. Symbol files contain what-
               ever information is needed to correlate the code and data in the executable
               with the source code. For example, the .pdb format used by Windows con-
               tains the following:
                    Symbolic information Function or variable names and their memory
                    locations
                    Source code information Filenames and paths, line number informa-
                    tion (mapping memory addresses to specific lines of code)
                    Type information Structure layouts, function signatures, type defini-
                    tions (enums, typedefs, and user-defined types)
                    Build information Compiler settings, version information, and time-
                    stamps related to the build process

 Choose the Right Type of Crash Dump
 Most crash dumps contain exception information (the reason for the
 crash), a call stack that includes registers, a list of threads, a list of modules,
 metadata about the CPU and operating system, and any active operating
 system handles (references to files, synchronization primitives, processes,
 and so on). They also include memory, which contributes the most to their
 size. You can leave out all or some of it, but you may inadvertently exclude
 important information by doing so.
      On Windows, you can choose between a full dump, which includes all
 the accessible memory, or a mini dump, which only contains memory that
 local variables on the stack directly or indirectly reference. You can specify
 what data you want when calling MiniDumpWriteDump().
      So far, all of the discussion has been about CPU-side issues, but graph-
 ics processing unit (GPU) crash dumps also exist on some proprietary sys-
 tems. Nvidia’s GPUs, for instance, offer Nsight Aftermath (https://developer
 .nvidia.com/nsight-aftermath).

 Turn Crash Dumps into Actionable Data
 Once you implement crash dump creation, you should look into access. Make
 it as easy as possible to get the most information from your users. At the very
 least, the program should tell them that a crash occurred and the location of
 the crash dump. It could do this the next time a user starts it after a failure.
       Your software should also share those crash dumps automatically with
 you. Shared network directory storage is straightforward and lets you regu-
 larly pick up the files.

                    A better solution is to upload them to a shared online service. This
               solution works for anyone independent of where they are in the world and
               which network they’re on.
                    Once you get an online service that collects your crash dumps, you have
               many more possible options than just storing them. The service can analyze
               your files and extract information so it can display them conveniently and you
               can avoid opening each one just to see where the program failed. Note that
               the online service also needs access to associated symbols for each executable.
                    The next step is to group crashes that happened in the same or a simi-
               lar place. That gives you more data for each bug and lets you draw statistical
               patterns, such as which crashes occur most often, how stable each release is,
               and whether a problem recurs after being marked fixed.
                    These kinds of statistics will improve your program’s overall quality by
               helping you focus on important issues. Looking at the call stack and crash
               frequency in a dashboard makes your debugging process easier.
                    To facilitate this workflow even more, you may want to add additional
               information such as the program’s log (see “Instrument the Program to
               Report Problems” on page 62) or let the user specify what they were doing
               when the crash occurred. You should track information like the CPU
               type, graphics card, and amount of installed RAM. Status reports like
               the amount of free memory at the time of the crash can also be helpful.
               Patterns from all this data could reveal information about the bug’s cause,
               indicating factors such as out-of-memory problems or specific CPU issues.

               Use Third-Party Tools to Simplify Crash Reporting
               While the basic implementation of crash dumps is relatively straightfor-
               ward, it can quickly become complex if you want one that works across all
               your supported platforms, collates reports, and provides actionable insights.
               Since you don’t often use crash reporting as a selling point, it’s rare to
               spend much time on it.
                    Luckily, third-party libraries and services can take most of the complex-
               ity off your hands. Such solutions can have a wide variety of feature sets.
               Some only generate crash dumps, while others offer full-fledged online fea-
               tures that provide lots of statistics and insights.
                    The very first step you should take is checking whether your target
               platforms already offer such services. Quite a few proprietary platforms
               do. They’ll have the most knowledge about their low-level components and
               access to data and features that might not be available to you.
                    Windows Error Reporting (WER) can generate a crash dump for any
               program (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/wer/windows-error
               -reporting) and automatically upload it if you’ve signed your software prop-
               erly. Getting access to these reports can be a bit tricky, though.
                    Open source libraries can also take most of the work off your hands.
               Google has two: the bare-bones, in-process Breakpad (https://chromium.google
               source.com/breakpad/breakpad) and Crashpad (https://chromium.googlesource.com
               /crashpad/crashpad), which generates crash dumps outside processes and
               facilitates uploading and sharing.
